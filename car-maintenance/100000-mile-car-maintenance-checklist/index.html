On the radar

    Monthly habits

      What matters after 100,000.

      At 100,000 miles, the risk shifts from “did I change the oil?” to “what is about to fail?” The planner still schedules routine work, but the inspection list gets more attention: brake lines, coolant hoses, belts, suspension bushings, tire age, battery health, and leaks.

      Prioritize safety and records

      • Brake lines, pads, rotors, and fluid; a soft pedal or pull is an inspect-now symptom.
      • Tires by age and condition, not just tread depth; check the DOT date code.
      • Coolant, transmission, and power-steering fluids; use the specified fluid.
      • Battery and charging system; a weak battery can strand a perfectly healthy engine.
      • Timing belt if equipped; a broken belt on an interference engine is a terminal event.

      Keep receipts and mileage in the glovebox. When you sell, a documented 100k service history is worth more than a clean exterior.

      Good questions.

      Short answers. The owner’s manual still wins.

      What maintenance should a 100,000-mile car have?

      At a minimum: oil and filters, brake inspection and fluid, coolant, transmission service if due, belts and hoses, battery test, suspension, tires, and a brake-line/underbody inspection. Any deferred repairs should be priced before a long trip.

      Is a 100,000-mile car worth maintaining?

      Often yes, especially with records and no structural rust. A mechanic’s inspection and a repair estimate are more useful than a generic rule. The cheapest car is frequently the one you already own.

      What often gets missed at 100,000 miles?

      Brake fluid, cabin filters, transmission fluid, coolant condition, brake lines, and suspension bushings. These are easy to defer because they do not always produce a dramatic symptom.

      Can I use this for a high-mileage EV?

      Yes. Choose EV and the engine-only jobs disappear. Focus on tires, brakes, cabin filters, coolant, suspension, charging equipment, and the 12V battery.
